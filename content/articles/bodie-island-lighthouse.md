The lighthouse most people photograph on the Outer Banks is Cape Hatteras. The
one you can actually climb this year is Bodie Island, twenty minutes south of
Nags Head, standing on open marsh with a boardwalk running out behind it.

It is a working tower with a first order Fresnel lens, and the climb is genuinely
a climb. 219 steps, no lift, and a balcony at the top with nothing between you
and the sound but a railing.

## The tower you see now is the third one

Two lighthouses failed here before this one worked. The first, built in 1848 at
54 feet, was put up without a proper foundation and began to lean and sink. The
second, finished in 1859 at 80 feet, lasted two years: Confederate forces
destroyed it in May 1861 to stop the Union using it.

The current tower was lit on October 1, 1872. It stands 156 feet, carries a first
order Fresnel lens, and wears the black and white bands that tell sailors which
light they are looking at. It was rehabilitated between 2009 and 2012 and opened
to climbers in April 2013, which is recent enough that plenty of locals still
remember when you could only look at it.

![The full black and white banded tower rising above the treeline against blue sky](/images/bodie-tower-full.jpg "The 1872 tower, 156 feet, lit on October 1 that year. Photo by Ken Lund, CC BY-SA 2.0.")

## Climbing it

219 steps to the balcony. The staircase spirals up the inside of the tower with
landings along the way, and most reasonably fit people manage it without drama,
though you will feel it. There is no lift and no way to skip the stairs.

The rules are firm and the rangers enforce them. You must be at least 42 inches
tall and under 260 pounds. Children under 12 need an adult 16 or older with them,
and no child may be carried. No bare feet, and no heels taller than an inch and a
half. It is worth measuring a keen seven year old at home rather than finding out
at the door.

![The upper section of the tower and the railed gallery that climbers come out onto](/images/bodie-gallery.jpg "The gallery at the top. The railing is all that stands between you and the marsh. Photo by Ken Lund, CC BY-SA 2.0.")

## Tickets are a morning job

This is the part that catches people. Climb tickets are sold on Recreation.gov
only, only for the same day, and they are released at 7 a.m. that morning. There
is no advance booking and no ticket window at the lighthouse.

In July and August the morning slots go quickly. Set an alarm, book from your
phone before you get up, and treat the rest of the day as flexible. Climbs run
every 20 minutes from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. through the season, which in 2026 is April
22 to October 12. There are also Thursday evening climbs from June 11 to October
1, and a full moon climb each month from May to September, both of which are
worth the effort if you can get on one.

If you cannot get a ticket, the climb line is (252) 475-9054.

![The welcome sign at the entrance with the tower standing behind it](/images/bodie-entrance-sign.jpg "The turn off Highway 12. Parking and the grounds cost nothing. Photo by Ken Lund, CC BY-SA 2.0.")

## The keepers' quarters are still shut

The Double Keepers' Quarters, the white building beside the tower that normally
holds the visitor center and bookstore, has been closed since an electrical fire
on January 7, 2025. Repair work began in early March 2026. No reopening date has
been announced.

Plan around it. There is no bookstore, and the restroom situation is not what it
was, so use the facilities before you drive down. The building is still worth
looking at from outside: it is a handsome piece of 1870s government architecture,
and the fire damage is not visible from the lawn.

![The white two storey keepers' quarters with the lighthouse standing beyond it](/images/bodie-keepers-quarters.jpg "The Double Keepers' Quarters, closed since the January 2025 fire. Photo by Mark Ittleman, CC BY 2.0.")

## The marsh is half the reason to come

A boardwalk runs from the parking area out into the marsh behind the station, and
it is free, open all year, and takes about ten minutes to walk. It is flat and
easy, which makes it the part of the site that works for people who are not
climbing anything.

Come early or late and it is one of the better birding spots on this stretch of
coast. Herons, egrets and ibis work the shallows, and the light across the grass
at either end of the day is better than anything you will get at midday.

![A wooden boardwalk running out through tall marsh grass toward the lighthouse](/images/bodie-marsh-boardwalk.jpg "The boardwalk is flat, free and open even when the tower is closed. Photo by John Buie, CC BY 2.0.")

## How the site changed

The station was automated in 1940, which ended the keepers' era. The photograph
below was taken by the Coast Guard four years later, before the parking lot
existed, and it shows how isolated the place was: a tower, two buildings, and
sand.

Cape Hatteras National Seashore took the light on in 2000, and the National Park
Service has run it since.

![A 1944 black and white aerial photograph of the light station surrounded by open ground](/images/bodie-aerial-1944.jpg "The station in 1944, four years after automation and before the parking lot. U.S. Coast Guard photo, public domain.")

## Practical notes

The grounds, the parking and the boardwalk are free, and there is no entrance fee
for Cape Hatteras National Seashore. Only the climb costs money.

Allow an hour if you are climbing, half that for the grounds and the boardwalk.
The lot is not large and fills mid-morning in summer. The park office is on
(252) 473-2111.

One more thing worth knowing if you are planning a lighthouse trip: Cape Hatteras
Lighthouse is closed for climbing and is not expected to open in 2026. Bodie
Island is the one to aim at.

Seasons and prices move, so check the
[official National Park Service page](https://www.nps.gov/caha/planyourvisit/bils.htm)
before you drive down.
