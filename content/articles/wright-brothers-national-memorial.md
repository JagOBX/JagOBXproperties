Most national parks ask you to imagine what happened. This one does not. The
field is still a field, the distances are marked in stone where they actually
fell, and the whole thing took twelve seconds.

That is the surprise of the place. You arrive expecting a museum and you get a
piece of measured ground.

## What actually happened here

On the morning of December 17, 1903, Orville Wright lay flat on the lower wing
of a machine the brothers had built themselves, released a wire, and travelled
120 feet in twelve seconds at a height of about eight feet. It was 10:35 a.m.

They flew three more times that day, alternating pilots. The distances went 120
feet, then 175, then 200, and then, with Wilbur flying, 852 feet in 59 seconds.
Then a gust caught the Flyer on the ground and damaged it badly enough that it
never flew again.

The photograph everyone knows was taken by John T. Daniels, one of the men from
the nearby lifesaving station who had come to help move the machine. Five people
watched the first powered flight in history. That was the entire audience.

![The 1903 Flyer just off the ground with Wilbur running at the wingtip](/images/wright-first-flight-1903.jpg "Five people saw it happen. Photo by John T. Daniels, public domain.")

## The flight line is the real exhibit

Walk out past the visitor center and you reach a granite boulder sitting on the
grass. That is the takeoff point, placed in 1928 for the 25th anniversary, at a
ceremony that drew more than 3,000 people including Orville Wright himself and
Amelia Earhart. A replica of the 60 foot launching rail runs beside it, because
the sand was too soft for wheels and the Flyer had to slide.

From the boulder, four numbered markers run away from you across the field. Each
one stands where a flight ended.

![The takeoff boulder with the numbered flight markers running away across the grass](/images/wright-flight-line.jpg "The boulder marks the takeoff point. The markers beyond it mark where each flight ended. Photo by rickpilot_2000, CC BY 2.0.")

## The first marker is closer than you expect

This is the part that lands. You stand at the boulder, look at the first marker,
and it is barely down the lawn. One hundred and twenty feet is shorter than a
passenger jet. People walk it in about twenty seconds, which is longer than the
flight took.

Then you keep walking to the fourth marker, 852 feet out, and the scale of what
changed in one morning starts to make sense. Take the walk. Reading the numbers
on a sign is not the same as covering the distance on foot.

![A stone marker carved with the words distance 120 ft, Dec 17 1903, pilot Orville](/images/wright-120ft-marker.jpg "Each marker carries the distance, the date and the pilot. Photo by rickpilot_2000, CC BY 2.0.")

## The monument on the hill

Kill Devil Hill is the 90 foot dune the brothers used for their glider tests
before they ever added an engine. It used to wander like the dunes at Jockey's
Ridge. It was planted and stabilised so the monument could sit on it.

The monument is 60 feet of North Carolina granite, dedicated in November 1932,
shaped a little like a wing and cut with the words "in commemoration of the
conquest of the air." A paved path takes you up. It is a short climb and worth
doing even if you skip everything else.

![The granite monument standing on top of Kill Devil Hill against a bright sky](/images/wright-monument-tower.jpg "Sixty feet of granite, dedicated in 1932. Photo by Ken Thomas, public domain.")

## The view from the top

From the base of the monument you can see the whole flight field laid out below,
the visitor center, the reconstructed camp, and the line of markers you just
walked. On a clear day you can see water on both sides of the island.

It is also the best place to understand why they came here at all. Steady wind,
soft ground to land on, and almost nobody around to watch them fail.

![Looking down over the flight field and the memorial grounds from the top of the hill](/images/wright-view-from-hill.jpg "The whole flight field from the top of Kill Devil Hill. Photo by Fritzmann2002, CC BY-SA 4.0.")

## The camp buildings

Two wooden sheds sit near the flight line, rebuilt from the brothers'
photographs. One is the hangar the Flyer lived in. The other is where they slept,
cooked and waited out the weather.

Look inside and the ambition of the thing gets clearer. These are plain wooden
sheds in the middle of nowhere. The brothers ran a bicycle shop in Ohio and
funded the work themselves, coming down for a few weeks a year, and this was the
whole operation.

![Inside one of the reconstructed wooden camp sheds looking out the open door](/images/wright-camp-hangar.jpg "The rebuilt hangar. The Flyer lived in a shed like this. Photo by Ken Lund, CC BY-SA 2.0.")

## The visitor center

The visitor center holds a full size reproduction of the 1903 Flyer, which is
larger than photographs suggest, plus exhibits on how the brothers solved
control when everyone else was chasing power. Rangers give talks through the day
and they are genuinely good. Ask when the next one starts.

The building itself is a listed landmark, a 1960 modernist design that became a
National Historic Landmark in 2001. There is a bookstore and this is where the
restrooms are.

![The low modernist visitor center building seen across the grounds in warm light](/images/wright-visitor-center.jpg "The 1960 visitor center is a National Historic Landmark in its own right. Photo from the Historic American Buildings Survey, public domain.")

## Practical notes

Entry is $10 per person aged 16 and over and it is good for repeat entry on the
days it covers. Under 16s are free. **The park takes cards only and does not
accept cash**, which catches people out at the gate. You can also buy ahead on
Recreation.gov.

Go early or late. Mid-morning through mid-afternoon in July is the busiest, and
the flight line has no shade at all. Bring water and a hat.

There is a working airstrip on site, First Flight Airport, with a 3,000 foot
runway open in daylight only and no fuel or services. Small aircraft still land
here, which is a good detail to watch for.

Hours and fees can change, so it is worth checking the
[official National Park Service page](https://www.nps.gov/wrbr/index.htm)
before you drive over. The park office is on (252) 473-2111.
