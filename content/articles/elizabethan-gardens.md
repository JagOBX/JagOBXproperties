The plan was a two acre garden showing what a successful colonist might have
planted, if the colony had succeeded. What got built instead is ten acres of
formal garden holding Renaissance Italian statuary that came within one phone
call of going to the Metropolitan Museum.

The idea started in 1950, with a group standing at Fort Raleigh watching The
Lost Colony. The Garden Club of North Carolina took it on in 1951 and leased the
land for ninety nine years. Then a contractor in Fayetteville mentioned that he
was dismantling garden statuary on the Whitney estate in Thomasville, Georgia,
and that Mr Whitney was thinking of giving it to the Met. It came to Roanoke
Island instead: an Italian fountain and pool with balustrade, a wellhead, a
sundial, birdbaths, stone steps and benches, all of it older than Elizabeth I.

The gift was so far beyond the original ambition that the design had to be torn
up and started again. Innocenti and Webel of New York were brought in to draw
the replacement. Construction began on 2 June 1953, the day Elizabeth II was
crowned, and the Gardens opened on 18 August 1960, the 373rd anniversary of
Virginia Dare's birth.

## What the ten acres actually hold

This is a garden of rooms rather than a long walk. The sunken garden is the
formal centrepiece, parterre hedging around the Italian fountain, and it is the
part that photographs the way people expect a garden on this coast not to.

The statuary is the reason to slow down. A monumental bronze of Queen Elizabeth
I stands under the trees, and the marble figure of Virginia Dare by Maria Louisa
Lander is set on her own in the shade a short way off. Between them sit the
Whitney pieces, which are the genuinely old things here and are easy to walk
straight past because nothing shouts about them.

Because it is planted for succession rather than for one big display, there is
no bad month to come except the ones when it is shut. Annual colour goes in
every season on top of the permanent horticultural collections.

## It is not Fort Raleigh, and that costs people money

These two share an entrance off National Park Drive, and visitors mix them up
every single day.

**Fort Raleigh National Historic Site is federal and free.** The Elizabethan
Gardens is a private 501(c)(3) sitting on ten acres inside the park boundary,
and it charges its own admission: $12 for ages 13 and over, $8 for ages 3 to 12,
free for 2 and under. The Lost Colony outdoor drama is a third separate
organisation on the same site with its own tickets again.

Knowing that in advance turns a confusing afternoon into a good one. Park once,
walk the free earthworks and the Hariot trail at Fort Raleigh, then pay to go
through the garden gate. Our [Fort Raleigh guide](/articles/fort-raleigh/)
covers the free half.

One more thing that genuinely sets this place apart: **a dog is $5 and welcome.**
Almost nothing else you pay to enter on this coast will take one. Read their dog
policy first, but if you are travelling with a dog and looking for something to
do that is not the beach, this is close to the only paid option.

## Hours, the January problem, and the sound shore

The address is 1411 National Park Drive, Manteo, and the number is
(252) 473-3234.

In 2026 the Gardens run February to 19 November, Monday to Saturday 9 a.m. to
5 p.m. and Sunday 11 a.m. to 5 p.m. After the time change on 2 November they
close at 4 p.m. daily.

Now the parts that ruin trips. **The Gardens are closed for the entire month of
January.** From 20 November to 29 December daytime general admission stops
completely, and the only way in is WinterLights on select evenings from 6 p.m.
to 9 p.m. They are also shut on Thanksgiving and on 24 and 25 December. So a
winter visit is either an evening lights ticket or nothing, and in January it is
nothing.

Two smaller traps. Last tickets are sold an hour before closing and last entry
is 45 minutes before, so turning up at ten to five in July does not get you in.
And tickets are non refundable unless the Gardens close, which includes closing
for forecast winds above 35 mph. On this coast that is a real forecast, not a
formality.

Walk to the far edge before you leave. The property runs down to the sound, and
two brick pillars stand out on the shoreline there, the remains of an old pier
that erosion has taken apart. It is the least manicured thing on the property
and it tells you more about where you are standing than the hedges do.

Hours, dates and prices are set by the Gardens and change, so check the
[official admission page](https://www.elizabethangardens.org/admission-hours-tickets/)
before you drive over.
