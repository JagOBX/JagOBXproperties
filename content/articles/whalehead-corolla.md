Duck hunting lodges are supposed to look like duck hunting lodges. This one is a
yellow mansion with a copper roof on 39 acres of Currituck Sound, and the
National Register nomination for it reaches for three styles at once: dramatic
Beaux Arts form, with Arts and Crafts and Art Nouveau detail.

The reason it exists is better than the architecture. Edward Collings Knight Jr,
a wealthy northerner, wanted a hunting retreat on the Currituck flyway that his
wife could come to. The Lighthouse Club just up the road was men only and would
not have her. So he bought land and built his own, and the couple spent the
hunting seasons of 1922 to 1925 on site supervising the work. They called it
Corolla Island.

The name it goes by now came later and is nobody's design decision. Ray T Adams
bought the place in 1940, and by tradition a whale bone turned up while land was
being cleared for an airstrip. Whalehead Club.

## Cork floors and signed Tiffany

Most of what survives from that era on this coast was built to be useful: a
lighthouse, a life saving station, a keeper's quarters. This was built to be
lived in expensively, in a village with no road to it.

The details are the reason to pay. The floors are half inch cork tile. The light
fixtures through the first and second floors are signed and numbered Tiffany,
and in the foyer they hang as drop sconces with gold coloured glass globes
shaped like morning glories. None of that concedes anything to where it is,
which is the whole charm.

The county has restored the house and runs it as a museum with rooms presented
as they were in 1925. An hour inside is about right.

## Four operations, one car park

This is the thing to sort out before you arrive, because it is the same
confusion Fort Raleigh causes down on Roanoke Island.

Historic Corolla Park is county land and free to walk. On it, or immediately
beside it, sit **four separate things with four separate arrangements**: the
Whalehead house museum, which is the $7 ticket; the restored boathouse; the
Outer Banks Center for Wildlife Education; and the
[Currituck Beach Lighthouse](/articles/currituck-beach-lighthouse/), run by a
different organisation entirely with its own climb fee. The
[Corolla Wild Horse Fund](/articles/corolla-wild-horses/) museum is a short walk
away in Corolla Village.

So the honest plan is to park once and decide what you are actually paying for.
A family who want the lighthouse climb and a picnic on the sound can do exactly
that and skip the house. A wet afternoon flips it: the house is one of very few
indoor options this far north.

## Hours, the weekend problem, and the road that took thirty years

The address is 1100 Club Road, Corolla, and the number is (252) 453-9040.

Here is the one that costs people a day. **The museum runs Monday to Friday,
9 a.m. to 4 p.m.** The schedule is seasonal and county holidays move it. Corolla
is roughly an hour north of Kill Devil Hills on a good day and considerably
worse on a summer Saturday, so a weekend drive up to see the inside of the house
can end at a locked door. Check before you set out. The park, the boathouse
grounds and the soundfront stay open and free regardless, which is what saves
the trip if you get it wrong.

While you are on the lawn, it is worth knowing why Corolla felt like the end of
the world until quite recently. In the 1950s the Olmsted Brothers, the successor
firm to Frederick Law Olmsted, were hired to plan a Whalehead Club subdivision
on this land, laid out around a highway extension coming up from the south. The
owner, Ray T Adams, died in 1957. The road did not open until 1984. For roughly
thirty years this was a finished plan waiting for a road, which is a fair part of
why the wild horses still have somewhere to be.

Admission, hours and seasonal closures are set by Currituck County and change, so
check the
[official Whalehead listing](https://northernouterbanks.com/listing/whalehead-in-historic-corolla/)
before you drive up.
