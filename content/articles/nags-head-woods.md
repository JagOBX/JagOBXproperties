Everything else on this island is sand, wind and sun. Nags Head Woods is
hundreds of acres of shade, and it sits behind the dunes of Jockey's Ridge,
which is exactly why it exists. The great sand ridge takes the ocean wind, and
in the lee of it oaks, hickories and beech trees have grown into a canopy that
looks like the mountains of the eastern United States rather than a barrier
island.

It is one of the largest remaining maritime forests on the East Coast. It was
made a National Natural Landmark in 1974, and protecting it was one of The
Nature Conservancy's first jobs in North Carolina.

## What is actually in there

Seven distinct plant communities, one of which, the maritime deciduous forest,
is globally rare. Over 550 species of plants have been recorded, from the
hundred year old trees down to tiny orchids.

For anyone who watches birds this is the best few hours on the Outer Banks.
More than 150 species have been recorded and at least 50 nest here. Prothonotary
warblers, summer tanagers and blue grosbeaks spend the summer raising young
before heading back to Central and South America. There are more than 50
amphibian and reptile species, over 20 mammals including river otter, and a
bobcat, which is uncommon anywhere along this coast.

The freshwater ponds sitting between the wooded dunes hold seven species of fish
and the rare water violet. Walk far enough west and the fresh water gives way to
brackish marsh and then to the sound, and you can read the whole gradient in the
trees standing dead in the shallows.

## Trails, parking and the thing that ruins people's morning

There are eight trails, from 0.2 miles up to 3 miles. Most are dirt with grasses
and exposed roots across the surface, so wear closed toe shoes and expect to
watch your feet. Download the preserve trail map before you go, because the
difficulty varies more than the lengths suggest. The trails are for walkers
only, no bikes.

One trail is genuinely accessible rather than nominally accessible. A 0.5 mile
loop of concrete and boardwalk, 6 feet wide, maximum 5 percent grade and
maximum 2.5 percent cross slope, with two observation platforms, an accessible
fishing platform and benches at the start and the halfway point. Its trailhead
is separate from the main car park. From the main lot, turn left on Ocean Acres
Drive, carry on to the stop sign and continue about 50 yards, and the signs are
there along with two accessible spaces.

Now the part that catches people. **The main paved car park has 16 spaces.** Not
160. In high season that is gone by mid morning and there is nowhere else to put
a car. Go early, or go at four in the afternoon when the beach crowd is heading
back for dinner.

Two more practical notes. There is no fee, but you are asked to register at the
outdoor information counter. And the toilets are in the administrative office,
which keeps weekday office hours and closes when staff are out in the field, so
do not plan around them.

One last thing that makes the walk better. Until the 1930s this was a village,
with homesites, churches, a school, a gristmill and a shingle factory. You are
walking through somebody's neighbourhood that the forest took back.

Hours and trail closures are set by The Nature Conservancy and change, so check
the [preserve page](https://www.nature.org/en-us/get-involved/how-to-help/places-we-protect/nags-head-woods-ecological-preserve/)
before you set out. The number is (252) 441-2525.
